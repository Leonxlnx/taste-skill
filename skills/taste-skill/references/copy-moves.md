# Copy moves

The move for each part of the page, distilled from a study of 53 professional sites archived between
2014 and 2021, before language models. Every example line here is written for this file, for made-up
clients: learn the move, never the line. Where a real brand made the move famous, its name stands
beside it with at most five of its words, so you can recognise the move, never to copy it. The rules
of `copy.md` hold throughout: the hero passes the five-second test, every heading has its noun,
buttons are a verb and an object, nothing is invented.

## The hero: 14 forms

Most professional heroes are 2 to 9 words, one clause, never two adjectives stacked. Choose the form
on purpose and write it on the voice card (`copy.md`, The copy notes). The h1 may take any form; the
lead or the eyebrow then says plainly what is sold and where or for whom, so a stranger still knows
in five seconds. A form that needs a fact gets it in the very next line.

| # | Form | Made-up example (h1, then the line that carries the category) | Learn from | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Greeting, self-introduction | "Hello, we're Brannock." / "Bike repairs on Elmgatan, Malmö, back in two days." | innocent, Glossier ("Hi! We're Glossier.") | the voice is the product: playful, hurried |

When the brief does not decide between them, deal it: count the letters of the brand name, divide by
the number of forms in the row, and take the form at the remainder's place (remainder 0 is the
first). Write the form and the reason on the voice card. A bare category label ("Card payments for
market stalls") is form 9 only when you chose it there; otherwise copy-lint WARNs it.

**The lead (the sub-line):** software and services: how it works and the main benefit, 15 to 30 words,
one fact or one mechanism, or one proof point the brief gives; never a row of adjectives. A place: the
category, the place and today's hours, 80 characters at most. Whatever the h1's form, the lead is
where the category is plainest.

## Section headings

- **A label with a claim under it** is the professional default, not the two-fragment heading: a
  heading that names the part ("Battery", "Rooms and prices") and one line under it that claims ("Six
  rooms, all with the lake on one side"). Apple ran product pages on it.
- **A verb and the thing** works where the reader does something: "Send invoices from the van", "Plan
  the week's shifts". The most common shape on small-business pages. Never a bare command ("Get
  started", "Come climb").
- **A full-sentence claim** carries the argument: "Pays staff on the same day".
- **A figure as the heading,** only a real one from the brief, and best beside a named quote that
  vouches for it (Basecamp paired its meeting figure with a customer's words): "212 routes, reset every
  Tuesday".
- **Questions** are rare: the FAQ, the close, or once as a kicker set up for a short answer ("Not
  enough light? The lamp clips on."). Never a run of them.
- **The story section may be odd:** a year, a question, a dry joke as its heading (innocent once called
  a section "How to go bankrupt, quickly"), with the facts right under it.
- **Mix the shapes.** A page whose headings all have one shape, or share a phrase, reads as a form
  [copy-heading-echo].

## About and story

1. **Start in a moment the brief gives, not a thesis:** the day the oven arrived, the first market
   stall, the season the river flooded the cellar. (Blue Bottle opened on 1683 Vienna; innocent on a
   festival stall; Warby Parker on a pair of glasses lost on a trip; Mother on lunch round one kitchen
   table.)
2. **Anecdotes, a timeline or questions, never value pillars.** "Our values: quality, community,
   passion" is the weakest about page there is (the weakest in the study, Sweetgreen's, was built on
   pillars).
3. **Failure and luck belong in the plot** when the brief has them: the first plan that did not work,
   every bank that said no, the landlord who said yes.
4. **Founders as people, not titles,** and only the names the brief gives: a trait and a habit, not a
   job title.
5. **Values as behaviours with numbers:** "We pay growers twice the market price"; a rule, not an
   adjective (Soho House proved who gets in with a nomination rule, not a word like exclusive).
6. **End in a modest present tense,** not a vision: "Eleven of us now, and the same oven."
7. **Say how to reach them:** the form, the street, the hours, the names the brief gives.

A portfolio's about: one line with three roles, one of them deflating ("designer, occasional writer,
lapsed illustrator"); what the person makes and for whom; one or two projects with dates; one sentence
of belief; a short list of interests that mixes high and low, from the brief; availability as a plain
date. Or none of that: pure facts in the third person, the way Paul Graham writes his.

## Features and how it works

- **The mechanism in verb-led steps:** "Forward the invoice. The app reads the total and the due date.
  It pays on the day you choose." (Vercel told deploys in three such steps.)
- **The outcome first, the feature as its proof:** "Nobody misses a shift change", then how the
  reminder works and the real time it fires.
- **A moment, not a use case:** "Your drummer cancels at six; the stand-in gets the set list at
  6:01."
- **Real values in the examples:** times, sizes, amounts, never "customisable".
- **The product doing the work:** UI fragments with the client's own data, names and numbers
  (`cards.md`); real commands and file names on a developer page. On tech the UI carries most of the
  words (`copy.md`, the tech word budget).
- **Names are copy too:** rooms, plans, blends and models the brief names, used the same way every
  time. (The Hoxton sold rooms as "Shoebox, Cosy and Roomy".)
- **Card length varies with the material:** a title (a verb and an object) and one to three
  sentences, about 15 to 45 words at most; a card the brief knows a lot about may run longer than its
  neighbours, one may be a single line.

## Proof

- **Only what the brief gives:** quotes word for word with the name and role or town, figures with
  their source, logos of real customers who may be shown.
- **Let others say the superlatives,** with full attribution: a critic and the outlet, a named
  customer and the company.
- **Rough beats polished:** a real quote stays as written, lowercase and all (Gumroad showed one in a
  customer's own lowercase).
- **Pair a figure with a named quote,** so each vouches for the other.
- **A rule instead of a claim:** "Every frame is brazed by the person who measured you" proves the
  craft without the word.
- **None is fine.** Craft, luxury and small places often show no testimonials: the photographs and the
  facts carry the page.
- **Never:** a logo wall of unnamed clients, "as seen in" with no outlet, a rating with no source.

## Prices, FAQ, booking: the plain zone

- **The tone drops to facts:** the reader is anxious here.
- **Perks priced and literal:** "Late checkout until 14:00 for 15 euro", "Bike hire 12 euro a day".
  (The Hoxton priced late checkout by the hour; Pinboard wrote its yearly price into the button.)
- **A risk reversal beside the button, only when true:** "Cancel up to two days before, free".
- **Admit limits in the FAQ:** "Android comes next year; today it is iPhone only."
- **Ask the buyer's real objection and answer it flatly:** "Can I take my data out? Yes: every table
  exports as CSV, any time." (ZEIT asked its own lock-in question out loud.)

## Buttons and the close

- **A verb and the real object** (`copy.md` rule 4): "Book a room", "Order a cake", "Start a free
  trial", "Tell us everything" (Ueno's).
- **Literal even when the register plays:** the button says what happens next (Oatly's oat drink
  locator was a joke that still said what it did).
- **A low-pressure exit for browsers** where the register fits: "See the rooms first" (The Hoxton let
  visitors say they were "just looking").
- **The close's heading says something new** (`copy.md` rule 5). Pick one:
  - the hero's promise with one real number: "Bread from 7:30 tomorrow, rye until it runs out";
  - the admission turned into a reason: "We only take twelve bookings a night";
  - the risk taken away: "Free to cancel until two days before";
  - one plain question, once a page, when the register allows it: "Staying in August?".
  Never the heading or the button of the section above it [copy-close-repeat], never only the
  button's own words [copy-close-ask]. The button under it keeps the main action word for word.

## Microcopy: where a voice is most welcome

Professionals put their best lines here, not at the end of paragraphs. Write these whenever the page
has them; a stock form error under a hand-tuned hero gives the game away. The form rules of `copy.md`
hold (a label says what goes in, an error names the field and what to type, the sent line names what
was sent in the button's words); the register shows in the words around them:

- **Form error:** "Mondays we are closed: choose a day from Tuesday to Sunday."
- **Sent message:** "Your table request is in. We answer before noon the next day." (the time from the brief)
- **Empty state:** "No notes yet. Your first meeting lands here when it ends."
- **404:** "This page moved. The menu and the hours are one click up."
- **Newsletter:** the sign-up line in the register ("A letter on the first Sunday of the month, the
  rhythm from the brief"), and its confirmation ("Subscribed. The next letter goes out on the first
  Sunday.").
- **Field hint:** "Only for this booking." (and only what the brief promises)
- **A cookie notice,** when the page needs one, in the register like the rest.

Learn from: Oatly's cookie banner (cookies "go nicely with oat drinks"), innocent apologising for a
nosy date-of-birth field, Hiut Denim's webcam note, Stumptown's "secret notes" newsletter, Aesop's
"Sincere thanks for subscribing", Wieden+Kennedy describing its Work link ("It comes first").

## The footer

Facts only: the legal row of `copy.md` (the brand, this year, Privacy, Terms, the credits line), the
street and district, the hours on a place. No sign-off, no pun built on the name. A human line, if
any, is plain courtesy (Basecamp wished its readers a good rest of the day) or a real fact, and it stays out of
the footer on a tasteskill page: put it in the sent message.

## Numbers and humour

- **Odd human numbers beat round big ones:** "eleven people came to the first tasting", "45 seconds
  per side", when the brief gives them.
- **Every number sits in a sentence,** beside the claim it proves.
- **Humour points at ourselves, never the customer,** and lives in brackets, asides and microcopy, not
  at the end of every paragraph. None on a serious purchase (money, health) beyond the microcopy.
- **Rhythm on purpose, once:** a run of short sentences that paints a scene (Dishoom's run of who sat
  where at breakfast), a list that ends on a surprise word, one very long sentence beside a deadpan
  short one.
