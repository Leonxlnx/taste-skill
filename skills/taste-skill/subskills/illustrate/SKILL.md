# Illustrate

1. Write what a viewer must recognise and one question the image should answer. Use supplied product photography, official game art or a real interface when it already answers that question. Skip speculative drawings when the reader needs an exact record of a person, site, defect or measurement.
2. Choose one visual language for the page. Flat cut shapes suit a world with overlapping land, water and figures. Single-weight linework suits assembly and attachment. A coarse pixel grid suits a game or a sampled sensor output. Isometric blocks suit spatial relationships whose sides matter. Write down the viewpoint, edge rule and light direction. Draw with the project’s palette and use its spacing, radius and display tokens around the art. When working alone, take these from existing headings, controls and surfaces.
3. For a device, use its real dimensions or a supplied drawing to scale it against a familiar object in the same plane. If dimensions are unknown, identify the working assumption in the build notes. Pick a front view for output or a three-quarter view for depth. Identify the two or three features that distinguish the item, such as a clamp, lens and connector. Draw the contact at the correct edge. If the screen and lens face opposite directions, add an aligned rear detail or a small side section. Label the view at its smallest use size. Mirror the attachment position when turning the product over. In section, draw both contact surfaces against the host. Draw a readable output of one named subject. Label illustrative output as a sample.
4. For a world, plan three depths. Make a landmark, something the player can steer or touch, and a navigable route between them. Draw four to eight reusable parts in a shared coordinate system. Give SVG parts IDs distinct from section anchors. Mass the land and water first, then add scale with those parts. Cluster ripples under a hull, trees on a ridge and wear at the landing. Leave the rest quiet. Give night an observable consequence, such as the lantern lighting the channel.
5. Reuse the hero’s parts for one section crop and a composed 1200 × 630 share master. Reserve clear space between the share title and the nearest shape. Keep any sample label legible in the exported card. Choose the phone crop separately. Check all four edges for blank bands and keep both ends of the action visible. Allow one meaningful state change such as day/night or a thermal palette. Use native controls with text labels. Start with finished art in HTML. For movement, animate transform or opacity using `--dur` and `--ease` (fallback 180ms, ease-out). Supply a still under reduced motion.

**Reject** mixed clip-art styles, unconnected gadgets floating near a phone, scenery with no sign of play, a colour change that hides the subject, or noisy texture masking empty geometry.

**Deliver** accessible SVG in the hero and section, a self-contained share master, and any small CSS needed. Use `<svg role="img" aria-label="…">` or `<img alt="…">`. Hide decorative crops from assistive technology when the caption covers them. Export the SVG master to a supported raster format when publishing a social preview. Use flat fills and at most one shadow tone per material. Keep each source asset under 60 KB.

**Check** with `node check.mjs <page-folder>`. It checks IDs, inline labels, part references, and the size and viewBox of the self-contained `share.svg` master. At desktop and phone size, point to the subject, its identifying feature and its action. Inspect every state and the share crop. See `example/index.html` for the worked result.
